NEMT Trip Documentation Guide — What Every Record Must Contain

Haulxify Team · April 1, 2025 · 9 min read

Trip documentation is the single biggest source of Medicaid claim denials for NEMT providers. Not coding errors. Not eligibility issues. Documentation — missing fields, unsigned manifests, illegible records, and incomplete trip sheets that cannot support the claim when it is reviewed. This guide covers every documentation requirement you need to meet before submitting a Medicaid NEMT claim.

3–4x
Higher documentation denial rate for paper-based trip records versus electronic trip sheets. The difference is not the information — it is completion discipline. Electronic systems enforce required fields. Paper does not.

What Every NEMT Trip Record Must Contain

Regardless of which state you operate in or which Medicaid managed care organization you bill, every NEMT trip record must document these core elements:

  • Patient name — exactly as it appears on their Medicaid ID
  • Medicaid ID number — verified at time of service, not at scheduling
  • Date of service
  • Pickup address — full street address, not just city or zip
  • Destination address — medical facility name and full address
  • Actual pickup time — time driver arrived and patient entered vehicle
  • Actual drop-off time
  • Odometer reading at pickup
  • Odometer reading at drop-off
  • Vehicle ID or license plate
  • Driver name and ID
  • Service level — ambulatory, wheelchair, stretcher, as authorized
  • Patient signature or documented reason why signature was not obtained

Every single field above is required. A claim submitted with any field missing or illegible will be denied or flagged for review. There are no optional fields in NEMT documentation.

The Patient Signature Requirement

Patient signature at delivery is one of the most frequently cited documentation deficiencies in NEMT audits. The signature serves as proof that the trip was completed and the patient was transported to the destination listed on the claim.

When a patient is genuinely unable to sign — due to cognitive impairment, physical disability, or medical condition — you must document a specific reason in writing. Acceptable notations typically include:

  • "Patient unable to sign due to cognitive impairment — [condition documented in authorization]"
  • "Patient transported on stretcher — physically unable to sign"
  • "Patient a minor — guardian signature obtained" (attach guardian name and relationship)

A blank signature field with no notation is never acceptable. It will generate a denial that cannot be appealed on documentation grounds — you simply cannot prove the trip occurred as billed.

Some MCOs accept a caregiver or facility staff signature in place of the patient signature when the patient is unable to sign. Confirm this policy with each MCO you bill before relying on it — not all accept third-party signatures without prior authorization.

Mileage Documentation Standards

Medicaid pays for actual mileage from pickup to destination. Your claimed mileage must be supportable by your trip records and must be consistent with the MCO's reference mileage calculation.

The two most important mileage documentation practices:

  • Record both odometer readings on every trip. The difference between your pickup odometer and drop-off odometer is your documented mileage. This must match what you bill.
  • Maintain GPS trip records. If your claimed mileage differs from the MCO's mapping system calculation, your GPS data showing the actual route driven is your primary defense. A route that adds mileage due to road construction, traffic, or geographic constraints is billable — but only if you can document why the standard route was not taken.

Mileage discrepancies over 10 to 15 percent from the MCO's reference typically trigger automatic review. GPS records resolve most of these reviews quickly. Without them, you are arguing against a computer.

Authorization Documentation

Every Medicaid NEMT trip requires an authorization — either a specific trip authorization or a standing authorization for recurring trips like dialysis or chemotherapy. Your trip record must link to an active authorization.

Authorization documentation requirements:

  • Authorization number must be recorded on the trip manifest before the driver departs
  • Authorization must be valid on the date of service — verify expiration dates for standing authorizations weekly
  • The authorized service level must match the service provided — an authorization for ambulatory transport does not cover wheelchair service
  • The authorized destination must match the actual destination — authorizations are facility-specific in most MCO programs

Operating on an expired authorization is one of the most expensive documentation errors in NEMT — the claim is typically unrecoverable even if the service was medically necessary and properly delivered.

Electronic vs Paper Trip Sheets

The practical case for electronic trip documentation is not about technology — it is about completion discipline. An electronic trip sheet with required fields cannot be submitted incomplete. A paper trip sheet can be turned in with blank fields, illegible handwriting, or missing signatures without anyone catching it until the claim is denied weeks later.

If you are still running paper trip sheets, the minimum process improvement is a manual checklist review before any paper manifest leaves the vehicle. Every required field, checked at drop-off — not at the end of the shift.

The transition to electronic documentation — whether through a dedicated NEMT software platform or a simple mobile form — reduces documentation denials by the 3 to 4x factor cited at the start of this article. For most NEMT operators, the software cost is recovered in the first month through reduced denials alone.

Audit Readiness: How Long to Retain Trip Records

Medicaid documentation retention requirements vary by state but the federal minimum is 6 years from the date of service or 3 years after the final payment on a cost report — whichever is later. Most state Medicaid programs match or exceed this.

Practically, this means every trip record from the last 6 years must be retrievable within a reasonable timeframe if you receive an audit notice. Paper records stored in boxes are technically compliant but functionally disastrous during an audit — retrieving specific trips across years of paper takes days and increases the likelihood of missing documentation.

Digital storage with searchable indexing by date, patient, and authorization number is the operational standard for audit-ready NEMT operations. The cost of organizing your records is trivial compared to the cost of an audit finding based on missing documentation.
