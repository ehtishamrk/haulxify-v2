How to Do IFTA Reporting Step by Step

Haulxify Team · March 4, 2025 · 10 min read

IFTA — the International Fuel Tax Agreement — is one of those quarterly obligations that feels simple until you are actually staring at your mileage logs at 11pm the night before the deadline. This guide covers the entire process from start to finish, written for owner-operators and small fleet owners who handle their own filing.

$500+
Minimum penalty for a first-time IFTA non-filing. Late filings carry a $50 minimum or 10% of net tax due — whichever is greater. Repeat failures can escalate to license suspension.

Who Needs to File IFTA

IFTA applies to qualified motor vehicles operating in two or more IFTA member jurisdictions. A qualified motor vehicle is any power unit used to transport people or property that either has two axles and a gross vehicle weight over 26,000 lbs, or has three or more axles regardless of weight.

If your truck crosses state lines and meets the weight threshold, you are required to hold an IFTA license and file quarterly. Operating without an IFTA license when required is a serious violation — not just a paperwork issue.

IFTA covers 48 US states and 10 Canadian provinces. Hawaii and Alaska are not members. If you only operate within one state, check whether that state has its own fuel tax program instead.

The Four IFTA Filing Deadlines

IFTA is filed quarterly with your base jurisdiction — the state where your truck is registered and from which you operate:

  • Q1 (January–March): Due April 30
  • Q2 (April–June): Due July 31
  • Q3 (July–September): Due October 31
  • Q4 (October–December): Due January 31

A zero-liability quarter still requires a filing. Missing the filing entirely — even if you owe nothing — is treated as non-compliance and triggers penalties. File even if you did not operate that quarter.

Step 1 — Gather Your Mileage Records

IFTA requires you to document total miles driven in each jurisdiction for each quarter. This is where most small operators run into trouble — not because the math is hard, but because the documentation is missing.

Acceptable mileage documentation includes:

  • ELD records with jurisdiction-level mileage breakdowns — this is the cleanest and most audit-proof source
  • GPS trip data exported with state-by-state mileage from your tracking platform
  • Driver trip sheets completed at every state line crossing
  • Dispatch records cross-referenced with odometer readings — acceptable but creates more audit exposure than electronic records

What auditors check first: does your total reported mileage match the difference between your quarterly beginning and ending odometer readings? If your ELD shows 14,200 miles but your odometer difference is 18,400, you have a discrepancy that will get flagged immediately.

Step 2 — Gather Your Fuel Receipts

IFTA lets you claim credit for fuel tax already paid at the pump in each jurisdiction — but only with proper documentation. A valid fuel receipt must show: date, seller name and address, fuel type, number of gallons purchased, and your unit number or vehicle plate.

Fleet cards and card-lock receipts generally satisfy these requirements automatically. Cash purchases at truck stops are creditable only if you kept the physical receipt — "I know I bought fuel in Ohio" is not IFTA documentation.

One common oversight: bulk fuel purchased and stored at your yard. This is creditable against your IFTA liability, but requires a separate dispensing log showing how many gallons went into each vehicle. Most small operators miss this and leave money on the table every quarter.

Fuel tax rates change every quarter. Always download the current rate sheet from your base jurisdiction before calculating. Using last quarter's rates — even accidentally — generates adjustment notices.

Step 3 — Calculate Your Net Tax

The IFTA calculation follows this sequence for every jurisdiction you operated in during the quarter:

  1. Calculate your fleet's average MPG: total miles for the quarter ÷ total gallons purchased
  2. Calculate taxable gallons for each state: that state's miles ÷ average MPG
  3. Subtract actual gallons purchased in that state from taxable gallons
  4. Multiply the net gallons by that state's current fuel tax rate
  5. A positive result means you owe that state. A negative result means that state owes you a credit.

Add up all the results. If your total is positive, you pay your base jurisdiction. If it is negative, you receive a refund from your base jurisdiction — they settle with each state on your behalf.

Step 4 — Complete and Submit the Return

Most base jurisdictions now offer online IFTA filing through their motor carrier portal. The return asks for your total miles, total gallons, and per-state breakdowns. If you have done the calculation correctly in steps 1 through 3, filling in the return takes less than 20 minutes.

If you owe tax, payment is due at the same time as the return. Most portals accept ACH or credit card. If you are expecting a refund, it typically processes within 4 to 6 weeks.

The 5 Mistakes That Trigger IFTA Audits

  • Inconsistent MPG across quarters. If your fleet averages 6.8 MPG for two years and then suddenly reports 9.2 MPG in one quarter, auditors flag it automatically. Have an explanation ready if your MPG genuinely changes significantly.
  • High mileage in a state with zero fuel purchases. This is legal — you can drive through a state without buying fuel there — but it gets flagged. Make sure your GPS or ELD records clearly document the route.
  • Missing odometer reconciliation. Your beginning and ending odometer must match your total reported miles within a reasonable margin. Gaps of more than a few percent raise questions.
  • Filing for the wrong number of vehicles. If you added or removed a truck mid-quarter, your vehicle count changes mid-period. Most operators forget to account for this.
  • Late filing — even for zero liability quarters. A missing filing is non-compliance regardless of whether you owed anything. File on time, every quarter.

Software Options for Small Fleets

If you are running three or more trucks, manual IFTA calculation is not sustainable. TruckingOffice, Axon, and most modern TMS platforms generate IFTA returns automatically if you are logging mileage correctly in the system. For owner-operators, the IFTA reporting built into most ELD platforms — Samsara, Motive, BigRoad — is usually sufficient for quarterly filing.

The key is consistent mileage logging throughout the quarter. If you wait until filing day to reconstruct your mileage from dispatch records, you will spend hours on work that should take minutes.

What Happens During an IFTA Audit

IFTA audits are conducted by your base jurisdiction and typically cover four years of historical filings. Auditors request all mileage records, all fuel receipts, vehicle registrations, and driver logs for the audit period.

The most common audit outcome for small operators is not fraud — it is under-documentation. If you cannot produce records to support your reported mileage or fuel purchases, the auditor estimates using national average figures. Those estimates almost always result in additional tax owed, plus penalties and interest.

The records that prevent a costly audit cost nothing to maintain. They just require discipline throughout the quarter — not just at filing time.
