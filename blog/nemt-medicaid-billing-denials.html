The 6 NEMT Medicaid Billing Denials Killing Your Cash Flow (And How to Prevent Every One)

Haulxify Team · January 28, 2025 · 8 min read

Non-Emergency Medical Transportation is one of the most documentation-intensive segments in transport. Medicaid reimburses for NEMT — but only when every element of the claim matches the eligibility, authorization, and trip documentation requirements exactly.

Most NEMT operators have a denial rate between 8% and 15%. Operators with systematic billing processes typically run below 3%. The difference is process, not luck.

12%
Average Medicaid claim denial rate for NEMT operators without a systematic billing process. Each denial costs $45–$200 in administrative follow-up time — regardless of whether it gets reversed.

Denial #1: Eligibility Not Verified at Time of Service

Medicaid eligibility is not static. A patient who was eligible when they called to schedule may not be eligible on the day of transport. If you transport a patient whose Medicaid was inactive on the trip date, the claim is denied on eligibility grounds and typically cannot be appealed successfully.

The fix: Verify eligibility on the morning of the trip, not at the time of booking. Most state Medicaid systems have automated eligibility verification via EVS or through your state's MMIS portal. This takes 90 seconds per patient. Make it non-negotiable in your dispatch checklist.

Denial #2: Authorization Not Obtained or Expired

Many Medicaid managed care plans require prior authorization for NEMT trips. Operating on an expired authorization, or on a verbal authorization that was never documented, is one of the most expensive denial categories because the claim usually can't be recovered.

The fix: Build authorization tracking into your scheduling system. Every trip should be linked to an authorization number with an expiry date. Flag trips 48 hours before authorization expiry for renewal.

Denial #3: Incorrect or Missing Procedure Codes

NEMT claims are submitted with procedure codes that identify the type of transport: ambulatory, wheelchair, stretcher, etc. Submitting a wheelchair transport claim with an ambulatory code results in automatic denial. Service codes change — states update their NEMT billing guidelines, sometimes quarterly.

The fix: Subscribe to updates from your state Medicaid program and each MCO you bill. Review your code set every quarter.

Denial #4: Mileage Discrepancy

Medicaid pays for actual mileage from pickup to dropoff. If your claimed mileage is more than 10–15% different from the MCO's reference mileage, the claim is flagged for review or automatically adjusted down.

The fix: Use consistent mileage calculation. Document GPS-verified trip mileage for every run. Your GPS data should match what you bill.

Denial #5: Missing or Incomplete Trip Documentation

At minimum, NEMT Medicaid claims require: pickup and dropoff times, addresses, patient signature, driver identification, vehicle ID, and trip purpose. Missing any of these triggers denial.

The fix: Use a trip manifest or electronic trip sheet that prompts drivers through every required field with non-optional fields that can't be left blank.

Electronic trip sheets that sync to your billing software dramatically reduce documentation denials because they require completion before the trip can be closed. Paper-based trip documentation has a 3–4x higher documentation denial rate than electronic.

Denial #6: Duplicate Claim

Submitting the same claim twice — either deliberately or through system error — results in automatic denial of the second submission. This is more common than it sounds: if your billing software crashes mid-submission, or if a staff member submits without checking submission history.

The fix: Every submission should be logged with date, claim number, and status. Before resubmitting any claim, check the payer's provider portal for the original submission status.

What to Do With Denied Claims

Every denial generates a denial reason code (CARC and RARC codes on the EOB). These codes tell you exactly why the claim was denied and what the appeal path is. The appeal window varies by payer — typically 60–90 days from the denial date. Missing the appeal window means the revenue is gone.

Track your denial codes monthly. If the same code appears more than 3 times, you have a systematic process failure — not a random error. Fix the process, not just the individual claims.
